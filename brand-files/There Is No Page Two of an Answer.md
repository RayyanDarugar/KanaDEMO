# There Is No Page Two of an Answer: A Publisher's Playbook for AI-Era Visibility

*A guide for print, digital, and audio publishers*

**By Jessica Vose, Head of GTM at Kana AI · \[DATE\] · 6 min read**

Your audience development lead has a strange chart open. Rankings are fine — the health desk still owns its keywords, Search Console shows the same positions it showed last spring. Traffic is not fine. The line has been sliding for four quarters, and the gap between "we rank" and "they arrive" keeps widening.

The explanation is sitting one tab over, in the AI answer at the top of the results page. It cites three sources, answers the question completely, and the reader never scrolls past it. For twenty years, publishers competed for placement on a page of ten blue links, and being third or fifth still paid. An answer is a different kind of real estate. You're inside it or you don't exist, because there is no page two of an answer.
---

The scale of the shift is no longer debatable. Organic traffic to news publishers fell from 2.3 billion monthly visits in mid-2024 to under 1.7 billion by May 2025, while zero-click searches climbed from 56% to 69% of all queries (*Press Gazette / industry analysis, 2025*). Google search traffic to publishers dropped roughly a third in a single year (*Press Gazette, 2026*), and the AI chatbots consuming that attention send back less than 1% of page views in referrals (*9to5google, 2026*).

Publishers have spent two years responding with lawyers and licensing teams — reasonably, since 60% expect AI licensing revenue this year (*Reuters Institute, 2026*). But blocking and billing are only half a strategy. The other half is the one your search team will recognize, because we've run this movie before: a new discovery layer appears, it has rules, almost nobody knows the rules yet, and the publishers who learn them first own the traffic for a decade. That was SEO in 2003. This is the answer era's version, and here is the playbook.

## **Step 1: Establish where you actually stand**

You cannot manage a surface you've never looked at. Take the twenty questions your newsroom would say it owns — the queries where your coverage is genuinely the best available — and put them to the major assistants: ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Claude, and Google's AI Overviews. For each, record three things: were you cited, who was cited instead, and was the information attributed to you actually yours? That last column matters more than teams expect. Coverage gaps lose you a click; accuracy gaps lend your credibility to someone else's summary. Most publishers have never run this exercise, which means most of your competitors haven't either.

## **Step 2: Write like a source, not just a ranker**

The research on what earns citations is unusually concrete. A Princeton-led study of generative engines found that two techniques outperformed everything else: including relevant statistics and citing credible sources — together lifting visibility in generated answers by as much as 40% (*Princeton GEO research*). For publishers this is almost unfairly good news, because original statistics and named sourcing are what newsrooms produce anyway. The work is making sure it survives the format: put the number in the text rather than locking it in a graphic, name the dataset, date the claim. An assistant assembling an answer reaches for the clearest, best-evidenced sentence available. Make sure it's yours.

## **Step 3: Respect the freshness cliff**

AI systems weight recency hard — practitioners now talk about a three-month citation cliff, after which unrefreshed content quietly falls out of answers (*industry analysis, 2026*). That changes the economics of your archive. The evergreen explainer that anchored your search strategy for five years now needs a maintenance schedule: reviewed, updated, re-dated on a quarterly loop. Rank position could coast; citations expire.

## **Step 4: Treat visibility and licensing as one negotiation**

Your licensing team is deciding what the crawlers may take. Your audience team, if it's running Steps 1 through 3, is deciding what the answers should say. Those conversations belong in the same room, because they're both pricing the same asset. Knowing you're the most-cited source in a coverage area is leverage in a licensing negotiation; knowing a provider consistently uses your reporting without attribution is a different kind of leverage. Publishers who walk in with visibility data negotiate over facts rather than fears — and with deals like News Corp's five-year, $250M+ arrangement with OpenAI setting the benchmark (*Digiday, 2025*), the facts are worth real money.

## **Step 5: Make it a discipline, not a fire drill**

The tooling category for all of this barely existed eighteen months ago; there are now more than eight competing platforms and a search category growing past 22,000 monthly queries (*industry analysis, 2026*). That timing is the opportunity. When SEO professionalized, the publishers who had already built the habit — measure, adjust, re-measure — compounded their advantage for years while everyone else hired their first specialist. The answer era is at exactly that stage now. A monthly visibility review with the same seriousness your team once gave the search dashboard is what "early" looks like.

## **The pattern underneath**

Every step above is something your organization already knows how to do — audit a surface, sharpen sourcing, maintain an archive, negotiate from data. What's missing at most publishers is not skill but instrumentation: nobody can see the answer layer, so nobody owns it, so the response defaults to anxiety and outside counsel. The publishers pulling ahead have simply made the invisible surface visible and assigned it to someone.

## **Answer Layer Challenge**

Still skeptical? We're challenging audience and editorial teams at a variety of publishers to do the following:

1. **Run the twenty-question audit.** Your beat's twenty core queries, five assistants, three columns: cited, absent, misattributed. One afternoon of work, and it's your baseline.
2. **Date-check your top evergreen piece.** Find your most search-valuable explainer and its last substantive update. If it's older than a quarter, it's on the wrong side of the citation cliff right now.
3. **Shadow a competitor.** Put the same twenty questions through the same assistants and count their citations against yours. The delta tells you whether you're early, level, or already behind in your own coverage area.

Publishers learned to win a results page that had ten winners per query. The answer has one, sometimes three, and the contest for those slots is happening now, mostly unwatched. The publishers who put eyes on it first will own the front door of discovery — precisely because everyone else is still staring at rankings on a page readers stopped scrolling.
