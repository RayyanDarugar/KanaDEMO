# Grading Your Own Homework: Making Closed-Loop Reporting Your Advertisers Actually Believe

*A guide for retail and commerce media network teams*

**By Jessica Vose, Head of GTM at Kana AI · \[DATE\] · 6 min read**

The QBR is going fine until slide four. That's the one with the ROAS number your analytics team spent two weeks assembling — the clean-room join, the attribution windows, the transaction match. It's a good number. It might even be a conservative number. And the moment it appears, the brand's analytics lead asks the question you've heard at every renewal this year: "Walk me through the methodology."

She isn't being difficult. She's doing her job, because the number on the slide came from the same company that sold the media, measured the media, and would very much like to sell more media. You are, in the oldest sense of the phrase, grading your own homework — and your advertisers have started checking the grades.
---

The checking has numbers behind it now. 94% of advertisers say they don't fully trust retailer-reported metrics, and 71% rank incrementality — not ROAS — as their top retail media KPI (*industry research via AdExchanger, 2025–26*). Meanwhile the share of brands describing retail media networks as "a simple money grab for the retailer" has more than doubled in a year, from 8% to 19% (*Forbes, 2025*). They keep spending, for now, because the first-party data is genuinely good. But a budget that's spent skeptically is a budget that gets renegotiated hard, and with Amazon and Walmart capturing 89% of the industry's incremental growth (*eMarketer, 2025*), the networks that can't make their proof believable are the ones the remaining dollars leave first.

The encouraging part is that advertiser distrust is rarely about dishonesty. It's about four specific, fixable places where a closed-loop number loses the room.

## **Leak 1: The number has no outside anchor**

A ROAS figure computed entirely inside your walls, on your definitions, invites exactly the scrutiny it gets. The fix arrived recently: the IAB published formal measurement guidelines for commerce media in November 2025, including a standardized definition of incrementality — the causal lift a campaign produced, versus what would have happened anyway. Adopting the standard definitions, and saying so on the slide, moves the conversation from "your math versus ours" to "the industry's math, applied to your campaign." Standards only became available this cycle; adopting them early is a differentiator, not table stakes.

## **Leak 2: Attribution is doing a job only incrementality can do**

Most retailer-reported ROAS is attribution: the shopper saw the ad, the shopper bought the yogurt, the ad gets credit. Your advertiser's analytics lead knows that some of those shoppers were buying the yogurt regardless, which is why she asks about holdouts. And she has reason to push: when Albertsons Media Collective analyzed 42 campaigns, the choice of incrementality methodology alone shifted measured results by as much as six times (*Albertsons Media Collective, 2025*). Reporting an incremental figure next to the attributed one — with the methodology named — costs you a smaller headline number and buys you a believed one. Networks keep making the opposite trade, and it's the expensive one.

## **Leak 3: The same purchase is thanked three times**

Your advertiser runs on your network, on two other retailers' networks, and on the open web. When the same customer sees ads in all three places before buying, every measurement stack claims the sale, and the brand's own totals come out showing more attributed revenue than actual revenue. You can't fix the whole industry's double counting, but you can be the network that acknowledges it — reporting deduplicated views where clean-room collaboration allows, and being explicit about overlap where it doesn't. In a market where 88.3% of agency professionals say digital advertising needs more transparency (*Basis, 2026*), candor about the limits of your number is itself a trust signal.

## **Leak 4: The proof shows up after the decision**

This is the quiet one. Closed-loop reporting at most networks is a per-campaign artifact: someone joins impression logs to transaction data, someone validates the wrap report, someone builds the deck. By the time the proof exists, the flight ended weeks ago — and the budget conversation it was supposed to influence has often already happened. Brand advertisers demand proof that media spend drives actual sales, and the manual work of linking exposure to purchase across systems means the answer routinely arrives too late to shape the next buy. A defensible number delivered after the JBP is settled defends nothing.

## **The pattern underneath**

Look at the four leaks together and a theme emerges: none of them is a data problem. Retail media networks own the purchase-linked, first-party data that every other channel envies — that part of the homework is genuinely excellent. The leaks are all in how the proof gets produced: manually, per campaign, on your definitions, after the fact. Which means the trust crisis is really a production crisis. The network that can generate standards-based, incrementality-aware, transaction-joined reporting continuously — while the campaign is still running — isn't grading its own homework anymore. It's showing its work in real time, and letting the advertiser check it whenever they like.

## **Homework Challenge**

Still skeptical? We're challenging measurement and account teams at a variety of media networks to do the following:

1. **Clock your proof cycle.** Take your last completed campaign and count the working hours between flight end and a finished closed-loop wrap report. Then ask your sales lead when the advertiser's next budget decision happened. If the proof landed after the decision, that's Leak 4 in your own numbers.
2. **Run the second-methodology test.** Have your analytics team re-measure one recent campaign with a different attribution window or a holdout-based method. The gap between the two results is the credibility spread your advertisers are already discounting for.
3. **Ask five advertisers what they actually rebudget on.** Not what they receive from you — what they trust. If the answer is their own triangulation of your numbers, your reporting is being audited, and it's better to know.

Retail media's pitch has always been proof: the only channel that can draw a line from an impression to a purchase. The networks that grow through the skeptical era won't be the ones with the biggest ROAS on slide four — they'll be the ones who hand the grader the rubric, show the work, and never make a budget decision wait for the answer.
