# The 24-Hour Window: Where the Four Days in Your Proposal Process Actually Go

*A guide for print, digital, and audio publisher sales teams*

**By Jessica Vose, Head of GTM at Kana AI · \[DATE\] · 6 min read**

The RFP lands Tuesday at 4pm. The brief is decent, the budget is real, and somewhere in the email is the sentence that decides everything: responses due within 24 hours, 48 at the outside. Your planner reads it and starts a process that has never once finished in 24 hours — the avails pull, the rate-card email to the one senior seller who knows what this advertiser paid last year, the audience slides, the deck. Four days later a strong, thoughtful proposal goes out the door.

By then the agency has been reading responses since Wednesday. The shortlist formed around whoever showed up while the brief was still an open question, and your proposal — often the better one — arrives as a courtesy read. Nobody at the agency will say you lost on speed. But the window was 24 hours, and you weren't in it.
---

The cost of missing the window keeps rising because direct-sold is where publisher margin has concentrated. Private marketplace spend grew about 13% in 2025 as buyers moved away from open auction, and publishers like LADbible now take 54% of revenue through direct sales while open-web programmatic declines by double digits (*INMA / Madison Logic, 2025*). Every direct dollar starts life as an RFP response, which makes proposal speed a revenue lever hiding in plain sight. And the current cost of producing each response is documented: media teams spend around 38 hours and $3,018 per campaign on RFPs (*Bionic study*).

The way to fix a four-day process is to find out where the four days go. At most publisher sales desks, they go to the same four places.

## **Day one: the avails pull**

Before anyone can propose anything, someone has to find out what inventory actually exists — forecasted impressions by section, by format, by flight window. That means forecast pulls from the ad server, checks against what's already committed, and screenshots into a working doc. Here's the part that quietly ruins the schedule: avails expire. Inventory forecasts are typically only good for about 48 hours before they need re-running, so a proposal built at day-four pace is checking its own math against numbers that went stale on day two. Teams end up pulling avails twice for one proposal, and the second pull sometimes changes the plan.

## **Day two: the pricing chase**

The rate card says one thing. What this advertiser actually pays — the packaging exceptions, the value-adds, the floor the desk will really hold — lives in the heads of one or two senior sellers, and day two is largely spent getting on their calendar. This is the least visible bottleneck and the most damaging one, because it doesn't just slow the current proposal. It means the desk's best pricing judgment is a queue, and every planner who can't reach it either waits or guesses.

## **Day three: the audience story**

A differentiated media package needs more than impressions and a price. It needs the audience data that justifies the premium — first-party segments, category context, performance benchmarks from campaigns like this one. That story is assembled from analytics, the CDP or data team, and whatever the last similar wrap report said, which means pulling from three or four systems and often waiting on the one team whose queue is longest. This is the step where good proposals earn their margin, and it's also the step most likely to get cut when the deadline is already blown — so speed pressure quietly degrades quality exactly where quality pays.

## **Day four: assembly**

The last day is formatting: forecast screenshots into slides, pricing tables rebuilt by hand, the audience narrative rewritten around this brief. Pure retrieval-and-formatting labor, performed by the same planners and sellers who are supposed to be managing relationships and working the pipeline. When a desk says it can't scale proposal output without headcount, this is usually the day it's describing.

## **The pattern underneath**

Walk back through the four days and notice what's missing: selling. Almost none of the elapsed time is judgment, persuasion, or strategy — it's fetching things. The avails live in the ad server, the pricing lives in two people's heads, the audience proof lives in three systems, and the deck is where they all get manually reunited, once per RFP, forever. That's why the process resists every "respond faster" mandate: the bottleneck was never effort. A desk that fetches for three days will always lose the 24-hour window to a desk that doesn't fetch at all.

## **24-Hour Challenge**

Still skeptical? We're challenging publisher sales teams at a variety of desks to do the following:

1. **Timestamp your last five RFPs.** Received date, submitted date, and the deadline stated in the brief. Count how many responses landed inside the stated window. That ratio is your real win-rate ceiling, before pricing or product ever enter the conversation.
2. **Count the fetch.** For one recent proposal, list every system, document, and person a planner had to touch to assemble it. Each item on the list is a place the four days went.
3. **Ask your best seller for their split.** What share of their week goes to assembling proposals versus talking to buyers? Whatever assembly share they name is capacity your pipeline is paying for and not getting.

Agencies didn't shorten the window to be cruel; they shortened it because someone always answers in time, and that someone tends to win. The desks that grow direct revenue from here won't be the ones that write the best fourth-day proposal — they'll be the ones still inside the window when the shortlist forms.
