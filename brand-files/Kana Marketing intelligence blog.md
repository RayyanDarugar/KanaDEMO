But here’s a number most marketing leaders have never calculated: what it actually costs, in real dollars, to produce your weekly performance report. 

To give you the most obvious picture, we won’t be including the cost of the software, the media, or the data that feeds into it (even though that can rack up tens of thousands annually). ## **The math nobody runs**

A weekly marketing report, at a mid-size brand, usually involves three people.

An analyst builds it. All in all, call it **2 hours a week**: pulling exports from GA4, the retail media portals, Nielsen or Circana, the agency deck, and the CRM. Then running analysis, insight, data normalization. 

Finally they make a deck and pass it over to a brand manager for review and reconciliation: around **2 hours**. 

By the time you get it it’s Wednesday. You review, ask questions, and present it: **2 hours**.

Let's break down the personnel: according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the average marketing analyst in the U.S. makes around [$37/hr](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/business-and-financial/market-research-analysts.htm).  The average Marketing Manager makes around [$76/hr](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/management/advertising-promotions-and-marketing-managers.htm). A CMO’s average rate is around [$180/hr](https://www.gofractional.com/insights/rates/cmo). All together, that puts the blended, fully-loaded cost at around $97/hr.  
With $97 per hour, that's:  
(2 \+ 2 \+ 2 hours) × $97 × 48 weeks \= \~**$28,000 per year**

For one report.

Most teams don't run one report. They run one per brand, or per campaign stream, or per region. Run it across **three** and you're at:  
**$84,000 a year.**

That’s the price of a document that answers a Monday question by Wednesday and is stale by Thursday. And those assumptions above are conservative: they don't count the Slack threads chasing down why two systems disagree. They don’t count the software you bought for data. They don't count the rebuild when someone finds an error at 4pm.

## **Why the number is so high (and it's not the analyst's fault)**

When you see a number like that, your instinct is probably that your team is slow. They’re not. The real time suck isn’t in thinking and analysis, it’s in normalizing and moving your data.

Most of the analyst's time is transport: exporting data from one system, pasting it into a spreadsheet, normalizing it to the exported excel table structure, dropping it into a slide, and copying it into an email. Each one of those steps is a human hand carrying numbers between tools that were never built to talk to each other. 

And it gets worse by the time you deal with reconciliation. Your agency decks ROAS won’t match with the data you bought from your Retail Media Platform. Someone has to figure out why that’s happening every time before the report can go out, and even if it’s the same old attribution-window/counting-methodology difference, it’s still eating an hour of your life. 

The point being, a meaningful share of the $120,000 you have to spend each year is just resolving disagreements and moving data. Not learning, analyzing, or producing.

## **Why hiring doesn't fix it**

Your obvious move is just to add more headcount. An employee dedicated to reporting means faster turnaround and more capacity. 

In 2026, that marketing strategy is dead. A new analyst doesn’t change the architecture of your problem, it just runs the same broken pipeline faster. You’re not buying answers, you’re buying more hours of data transport at a higher annual cost. You’re using the same disconnected systems, with 6 sources, 4 translation steps, and 2 numbers that don’t agree. You’ve just hired someone to carry those buckets a little bit faster. 

**It’s not a staffing problem, it’s an architectural one.** Your report takes two days because your data is siloed and gets assembled by hand. Adding hands doesn’t fix that, removing the assembly will.

1. **Run the math on your own report.** Use the calculator, or multiply hours × people × weeks × rate. Whatever comes up is what you're currently paying for a stale answer.
