# 5 Churn Signals Your Retention Report Won't Show You (Until the Customer Is Already Gone)

*A guide for retail and e-commerce marketers*

**By Jessica Vose, Head of GTM at Kana AI · \[DATE\] · 6 min read**

Every retention marketer has the same end-of-month ritual. You pull the cohort report, calculate churn and repeat-purchase rate, flag the lapsed segments, and queue up the win-back sends. Within a day you know exactly who you lost last month.

That's the problem. By the time a customer appears in that report, they're already gone, and the report reads less like a warning system than **an obituary**. Every name in it was savable weeks earlier.
---

The math has never been less forgiving. Customer acquisition costs are up roughly 60% in five years (*LoyaltyLion, 2025*), and Shopify's 2026 Global Commerce Report tracked blended CAC climbing from $274 to $318 across 4.8 million merchants in a single year. Meanwhile, acquiring a customer costs anywhere from 5 to 25x what retaining one does, and Bain's famous finding still holds: a 5% lift in retention raises profit 25–95%.

In other words, the cheapest revenue in your entire P&L is the customer you already paid to acquire. Yet most retail teams measure churn the one way that guarantees they can't prevent it: monthly, in aggregate, after the fact.

The frustrating part is that churn is rarely sudden. It leaves a trail, and that trail runs through data you already own. Here are five signals sitting in your stack right now that the monthly report will never surface in time.

## **1\. The stretch between purchases**

Most churn happens gradually. A customer who bought every two weeks starts buying every three, then every five. She's churning *right now*, but your lapsed-customer rule (60 or 90 days without a purchase) won't catch her for another two months, because she hasn't crossed the line yet.

The way to catch her is to watch her rhythm, days since last purchase measured against her own history, rather than waiting for her to go fully absent. A monthly shopper going quiet for six weeks is a mild wobble; a weekly shopper going quiet for six weeks is a five-alarm fire. Global lapse rules treat them identically.

**What to do:** track how much each customer's gap between purchases is stretching compared to her own usual pace, not against a blanket calendar rule. Even a simple ratio of days since last purchase over her average gap, refreshed weekly, will surface at-risk customers a full cycle before any lapsed rule does.

## **2\. The second-purchase cliff**

Retail churn doesn't spread evenly across the customer lifecycle. It concentrates at one moment: between the first purchase and the second. Somewhere between 70 and 77% of first-time e-commerce customers never come back (*CRO Benchmark, 2025*). Get someone to a second purchase and their retention odds jump dramatically; fail, and the acquisition money you just spent is a write-off.

Your aggregate retention rate blends this cliff into invisibility. A "healthy" 74% overall number can hide a first-to-second conversion rate that's bleeding out.

**What to do:** break second-purchase rate out as its own KPI, and treat days 0–60 after a first purchase as its own campaign, with its own creative, cadence, and owner. It's the highest-leverage window in the entire lifecycle, and most teams run it on the same generic post-purchase flow as everyone else.

## **3\. Engagement decay that precedes purchase decay**

Before the wallet goes quiet, the attention does. Email opens fade. App sessions drop. Loyalty scans stop appearing at the register. These signals move weeks ahead of the transaction data, which makes them your earliest warning.

So why does nobody act on them? Because they live in different systems. Your email platform knows about opens, the loyalty platform knows about scans, the register knows about baskets, and 60% of B2C teams are juggling 6 to 15 marketing tools (*Klaviyo State of B2C, 2025*) that don't share one definition of the same customer. The at-risk shopper is visible in three tools and flagged in none.

**What to do:** you don't need a big project to stitch customer identities together. Start by pulling three signals per customer (last purchase, last email engagement, last loyalty activity) into one weekly view. When two of the three are decaying together, that's a real risk flag, not noise.

## **4\. The customer who only shows up on sale**

Here's an uncomfortable gap: 89% of executives believe their customers became *more* loyal recently. Only 39% of consumers agree (*PwC via NIQ, 2025–26*), and 60% of consumers say they've switched away from a brand they were loyal to over cost.

That gap hides inside your revenue numbers as a specific behavior: the customer who still buys, but only on promotion. Her frequency looks fine. Her revenue looks fine. But her full-price purchases have quietly gone to zero, which means her loyalty already left; only the discount hunting remains. The margin disappears long before the revenue does.

**What to do:** track what share of each customer's spend happens on markdown. When it crosses a threshold (say, 80%), stop sending her deeper discounts. She needs a reason beyond price, or she's gone the day a competitor's coupon beats yours.

## **5\. Averages that hide your best customers leaving**

Your blended retention rate is a number built to stay calm. It can hold perfectly steady while a disaster unfolds underneath it. Retail churn averages around 25% (*CustomerGauge, 2025*), but your revenue doesn't churn at the average. If a handful of your highest-spending customers quietly lapse while a wave of low-value one-timers technically "retains," the blended number barely moves, even though the revenue hit is very real.

**What to do:** break retention out by customer value, and put a specific alert (and a specific human) on your top 10%. Losing one VIP is worth more intervention budget than losing twenty one-time bargain hunters, but a blended report prices them identically.

## **The pattern underneath all five**

None of these signals require data you don't have. Unlike a CPG brand that has to buy third-party data just to see its own shoppers, a retailer *owns* the transaction record, the loyalty program, and the customer identity. Every signal above is already sitting in systems you pay for.

The failure is one of timing: churn gets treated as a monthly report instead of a live signal, and late detection leaves you exactly one lever, the blanket win-back discount. Which is the worst tool in the drawer, because it burns margin on customers who were coming back anyway and arrives too late for the ones who weren't.

## **Churn Prediction Challenge**

Still skeptical? We're challenging retention and lifecycle teams at a variety of companies to do the following:

1. **Rewind your last lapsed list.** Take 20 customers who hit "lapsed" last month and pull their purchase history. Count how many showed a stretching purchase interval 6–8 weeks before they crossed the line. That percentage is how much churn was visible, and savable, in advance.
2. **Audit your win-back with a holdout.** On your next win-back send, hold out 10% of the segment and send them nothing. If that group comes back at nearly the rate of the discounted group, you've been paying customers to do what they were already going to do.
3. **Find your second-purchase number.** Calculate what percentage of your last 12 months of first-time buyers ever bought again. If you've never broken it out from blended retention, prepare to be surprised.

The retailers who win the next few years won't be the ones with the most polished retention report. They'll be the ones who stopped reading obituaries and started watching vital signs.
